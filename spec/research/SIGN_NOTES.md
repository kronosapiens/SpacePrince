# Sign research notes

Consulted 2026-10-11 to develop the twelve signs as references for house encounters.
The [sign matrix](../concept/PLANET_MATRIX.md#sign-matrix) records each sign's emblem, element, modality, and ruler; the matrix's placement entries describe planets in signs for the Prince's introductions.
These notes ask a different question: what does a sign describe when it occupies a house — the people found there, the places, the season, and the manner of its matters?
They paraphrase sources with locators; nothing here is game writing, a scene, or an accepted design rule.
These are astrological interpretations used as literary material, not claims about what causes a person's character or circumstances.

Quotes were checked against downloaded copies of each text.
Omitted throughout: disease and medical claims; predictions of fortune, offspring, and life outcomes; lists of countries and cities; and claims resting on gender, ethnic, or national stereotypes.
The planet and sign matrix omitted appearance because it did not help introduce the Prince; descriptions of people are kept here because a house's sign can describe other people.
Every source's seasons are northern-hemisphere and agricultural: grain, hay, vines, and pigs.

## How a sign qualifies a house

### Lilly

- To judge the "condition, quality or stature" of a person, "observe the Sign of that house whereby he is signified, the sign wherein the Lord of that house is, and wherein the Moon is", then "mix one with another" (Book I, ch. XVII, p. 100).
  His example: a human, airy sign on the house, with its lord or the Moon in a sign of like nature, shows a handsome body and courteous, sociable conditions.
- For the querent, "the Sign ascending shall in part signifie his corporature, body or stature", while the ascendant's lord, the Moon, and planets in the ascendant show his conditions (Book I, ch. XX, p. 123).
  For any other matter, take its house, the sign on that house, and the sign's lord: where the lord stands, its dignity, and its aspects (pp. 123–124).
- The same procedure locates things: "The nature and quality of the place is knowne by the Signes the Significators are in" (Book II, p. 203).
  Airy signs put a lost thing high or in the eaves; fiery, near a chimney or wall; earthy, on the floor; watery, in a dairy, wash-house, or damp place (pp. 203–204).
- The sign on the Fourth describes land (p. 207).
  Fire signs give hilly, dry, hard ground; earth signs plain pasture or tillage; air signs ground neither very hilly nor flat; water signs a river or plenty of water.
  A malefic there colours it further: Saturn in a watery Fourth gives boggy, rushy ground, and in a human sign land mismanaged by its former occupiers (pp. 207–208).
- A young querent's trade follows the nature of the sign on the Tenth and its lord (p. 171).
- A go-between is described "according to the Signe wherein he is, and his quality from the house he is Lord of" (p. 317).
- A thief's complexion and condition come from the Seventh's lord and sign; a fixed sign shows one thief, a double-bodied sign more than one (pp. 331, 339).
- House position can override a sign's description.
  With Capricorn rising Lilly often found white hair, "but in the seventh ever Black", which he attributes to "the nature of the Family rather then of the Sign" (p. 98).
- The sign classes have their own uses (Book I, pp. 89–90):
  - a significator in a bestial sign gives the person "something of the nature of that Beast which represents that Sign";
  - human signs show someone "of civill carriage, very affable and easie to be spoken withall";
  - the ascendant and its lord in moveable signs show a wavering person, in fixed signs one who stands by what he has said.

### Houlding

- In a published judgement the Tenth's sign describes a querent's work: "She runs her own business in ladies' fashions (Libran Midheaven)" ([“Is there a future…”](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/future.html)).
- A lost ring was in the querent's luggage: "Mercury in Gemini is perfectly descriptive of luggage transported by hand", because Gemini signifies chests ([“Where is my sapphire ring?”](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/ring.html)).
- [“Horary: Where is it?”](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/wit.html) modernises Lilly's places and adds element and mode rules.
  "Humane signs … indicate that the lost object will be found in a place frequented by people"; moveable signs suggest high places and new buildings, fixed signs low and hidden places, common signs eaves, closets, or the home.
- Her course keeps the three references distinct: "Mars is not the same as Aries, nor is it the same as the first house" ([“Traditional Sign Rulerships”](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/horary1b.html)).
  A quesited matter "is described by the house that naturally governs that matter" and signified by the ruler of its cusp ([“Identifying Significators”](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/horary1a.html)).

### The Astrology Podcast, episode 231

- Austin Coppock: "there are not good and bad signs, whereas there are apparently more powerful and more favorable houses and inherently way, way rougher houses."
- Chris Brennan calls the equation of Aries with the First house a recent construction, made primary by Alan Leo and later 20th-century astrologers; Coppock says it "corrupts the signs" and counts "a hundred and forty-four overlaps" between the two sets of twelve.
- Kelly Surtees: every house has a ruler, so "you want to look at the unique sign-house combination in the chart you're looking at".
  "The sign on the second cusp is going to flavor your attitude" to holding or spending, and even the element or modality of the sign on the Fourth can describe what matters in a home.
- Brennan: before the 20th century "your sign" meant the rising sign, which qualified character; the house ruler's sign "will color some things about the first house".

### Ptolemy and Valens

Neither has a passage on how the sign occupying a house qualifies its matters.
Ptolemy's structure is analogous: the signs containing Mercury and the Moon "contribute much to the character of the soul" (III.13, p. 333), and the signs of the rulers of action "contribute to the variety of the action" (IV.4, p. 389).
Valens qualifies each sign's natives by the sign's relationship to its house ruler (I.2, pp. 9, 12).

**Seasons and their phases.**
Ptolemy calls the signs where the Sun turns solstitial and those where night equals day equinoctial; in the following solid signs the season "touch[es] us more firmly", and the bicorporeal signs share the weather of the season ending and the one beginning (I.11, pp. 65–69).
He maps the seasons onto the ages of life: spring youth, summer the prime, autumn past the prime, winter nearest dissolution (I.10, pp. 59–65).
Manilius instead counts each season from its double sign: spring from Pisces, summer from Gemini, autumn from Virgo, winter from Sagittarius (II.265–269).

**What each phase governs.**
Ptolemy assigns equinoctial signs to "sacred rites and the worship of the gods", solstitial signs to changes in the air and in political customs, solid signs to "foundations and the construction of houses", and bicorporeal signs to "men and kings" (II.7, p. 175).
For character, solstitial signs make souls fitted for public life, political, and glory-seeking; bicorporeal signs complex, changeable, versatile, and fond of music; solid signs just, "unaffected by flattery", persistent, stern, and tenacious of grudges (III.13, p. 335).
For work, human signs favour "all scientific pursuits or those useful to man"; four-footed signs mines, commerce, building, and carpentry; solstitial and equinoctial signs interpretation, barter, measuring, farming, and religion; terrestrial and watery signs work with liquids, plants, shipbuilding, burial, and salting (IV.4, pp. 389–391).

**Forms.**
Ptolemy's classes of eclipse effects include signs of human form for people, four-footed signs for beasts, winged signs (Virgo, Sagittarius) for birds, signs of the sea (Cancer, Capricorn) for sea creatures and "the sailing of fleets", and signs of rivers (Aquarius, Pisces) for river creatures (II.7, pp. 171–175).
He makes Leo, Virgo, and Sagittarius produce larger bodies; Pisces, Cancer, and Capricorn smaller; Virgo, Libra, and Sagittarius well-proportioned; Scorpio, Pisces, and Taurus awkward (III.11, pp. 315–317).
Manilius divides the signs into water, land, and mixed (II.223–233) and makes Gemini and Pisces the true pairs: the Twins hold each other arm in arm while the Fish swim apart (II.157–166).

**Disagreements.**
Barren signs are Gemini, Leo, and Virgo for Lilly but Virgo, Leo, and Aquarius for Manilius (II.234–243).
Rhetorius, in Robbins's notes, adds part of Sagittarius to the human signs.
Manilius's day signs (II.203–222) do not follow the masculine and feminine alternation he gives just before.

### Seasons and labours

Medieval calendars pair each month with the sign the Sun enters during it, January with Aquarius through December with Capricorn (Mâle, p. 68).
A sign spans parts of two months, and cycles vary: Chartres pairs January with Capricorn, and the *Très Riches Heures* shows both signs over each month.
Local timing also differs: Reims threshes in September, and Italian cycles run about a month earlier than those of the Low Countries and England.

## The twelve signs

### Aries

**Season.** Ptolemy makes Aries equinoctial and the starting point of the zodiac, with weather of "thunder or hail" (I.10–11; II.11, p. 201).
Houlding: the equinox began the ancient new year with festivals of renewed vitality, a theme carried by Easter's date and egg-giving (¶1–2).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: a head-butting ram whose quickly regrown fleece symbolised the Sun's return, and the golden ram that rescued Phrixus and Helle, its fleece later sought by the Argonauts (¶2, ¶6).
**Temperament.** Houlding: haste and directness; ambition to be first; impatience with people who expect their minds to be read; a quick temper without grudges (¶3, ¶7, ¶9, ¶11).
Valens: distinguished, governing, just, bold in thought, boastful, restless, and quickly changing (p. 9).
Manilius: always hopeful after loss, wavering, and eager to sell themselves by their own praise (IV.125, 138–139).
Lilly calls the sign "luxurious, intemperate and violent" (p. 93), and a significator here rash and hardy (p. 89).
**People.** Lilly: lean and big-boned, not tall; long face and neck, black eyebrows, thick shoulders, and a swarthy complexion (pp. 93–94).
Houlding: a reddish or well-tanned complexion and a lean, active build.
**Trades.** Manilius gives the wool trades, from winding and spinning to weaving and selling cloth (IV.128–132).
Houlding gives smiths and foundry workers, and "work involving heat, danger, action, aspirational challenges or chemical sciences" (¶16).
**Places.** Lilly: where sheep and small cattle feed; sandy or hilly ground; "a place of refuge for Theeves, (as some unfrequented place)"; newly enclosed or ploughed land; where bricks or lime are burned; in houses, the roof covering, ceiling, and plaster (p. 93).
Houlding adds construction sites, foundries, and the kitchen or anywhere near fire.
**Correspondences.** Head; red and burnt colours; sheep, goats, and other hoofed animals (Houlding).

### Taurus

**Season.** Ptolemy: solid, the spring felt most firmly (I.11).
Houlding: late spring, when growth has opened into colour and flowers (¶6).
Manilius: the sign calls idle fields back to tillage and leads the ploughing itself (IV.144–147).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: the domesticated ox of early farming, behind the wheel and the plough, and the sacred bulls of Crete and Egypt (¶2–4).
Manilius: the bull sits wearily with the plough lifted from its neck (II.244–255), and rises backwards (II.197–202).
**Temperament.** Houlding: easygoing and fond of pleasure; immovable under pressure, so led patiently rather than pushed; rare, explosive anger; loyalty; a love of tradition and of proven assets such as property (¶7–9, ¶11, ¶15).
Valens calls the sign "productive of order, earthy, rustic, related to farming", its natives energetic, toilsome, good at keeping things, pleasure-loving, and generous (pp. 10–11).
Manilius: a love of praise left unspoken; slow but strong (IV.150–151).
Lilly: a significator here is steadfast and resolved (p. 89).
**People.** Lilly: short but full, strong, and well set; broad forehead, big face, large shoulders, thick lips, heavy hands, and black rugged hair (p. 94).
**Trades.** Manilius: plain farmers whose reward is the yield, not fame (IV.140–142).
Valens: labourers and planters.
Houlding: farming and practical crafts such as pottery and cookery; singers (¶16–17).
**Places.** Lilly: horse stables; low houses; sheds for cattle gear; pasture far from houses; ground lately cleared of bushes and sown; in houses, cellars and low rooms (p. 94).
Houlding adds cottages and places near the ground marked by farming, wood, and mud.
**Correspondences.** Throat and neck; earthy browns and green; cattle and herded animals (Houlding).

### Gemini

**Season.** Ptolemy: bicorporeal, carrying spring into summer (I.11).
Manilius: one twin brings flowering spring and the other parching summer (II.181–196).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: Castor, mortal and quick-witted, and Pollux, immortal and athletic; when Castor was killed Pollux shared his immortality, and the twins alternate days between Olympus and Hades (¶2–5).
Houlding's Mercury presides over messengers, commerce, and roads, and also over jugglers, liars, and thieves (¶10).
**Temperament.** Houlding: curiosity broad rather than deep; seeing every side and mediating; sociable networkers called fickle and two-faced, but adaptable where demands conflict (¶8, ¶12–13, ¶15).
Manilius: gentler pursuits; labour itself is pleasure; they avoid weapons and keep a lasting youth (IV.152–157).
**People.** Lilly: tall and upright, with long arms, dark hair, a strong active body, and "a good piercing hazel Eye"; "of excellent understanding, and judicious in worldly affairs" (pp. 94–95).
**Trades.** Valens: "scholars, those working in education and letters, poets, music lovers, declaimers, stewards, those who receive trusts", and also translators, merchants, and judges (p. 12).
Manilius: singers, pipers, string players, and students of the stars (IV.153–159).
Houlding: reporters, teachers, messengers, interpreters, and traders; traditionally also conjurers and pickpockets (¶9, ¶13).
**Places.** Lilly: panelled rooms; walls and plastering; halls where play is used; hills and mountains; barns and corn stores; coffers and chests; high places (p. 94).
Houlding adds places near windows and, through Mercury, libraries and rooms for study, play, or keeping money.
**Correspondences.** Arms, shoulders, and hands; mixed colours; songbirds and talking birds (Houlding).

### Cancer

**Season.** Ptolemy: solstitial; the Sun turns and summer begins (I.11).
Houlding: the Sun seems to stand still before turning south (¶8).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: a hard shell over a soft interior; a sideways gait; a claw that grips money and feeling alike (¶1, ¶8, ¶11).
Manilius: the crab lies sprawled on its belly, eyeless, rocky in the waves (II.223–264).
**Temperament.** Houlding: tidal moods, absorbent memory, and avoidance of conflict; looking back to ancestry, genealogy, and family custom; clan-like, protective, and slow to loosen family ties; careful long-term investors (¶4–5, ¶7, ¶9–12).
Valens: ambitious, popular, theatrical, party-giving, and easily downcast; "Unsteady of mind, they say one thing but think another" and "become wanderers and travelers" (pp. 13–14).
Manilius: tenacious, spending on nothing, and fighting for their gains (IV.165, 175).
Lilly calls the sign "mute and slow of Voyce" (p. 95).
**People.** Lilly: low and small, the upper body bigger than the lower; a round face, pale complexion, dark brown hair, and little eyes (p. 95).
**Trades.** Manilius: merchants who carry goods across seas and watch for grain shortages, or at home, moneylenders (IV.166–174).
Houlding: sailors and business managers.
**Places.** Lilly: the sea, great rivers, and navigable waters; brooks, springs, and wells; marsh ground, ditches with rushes, and sea banks; in houses, cellars, wash-houses, and cisterns (p. 95).
Houlding adds ships, fisheries, ale-houses, breweries, and taverns.
Ptolemy makes Cancer a sign of the sea, concerned with sea creatures and "the sailing of fleets" (II.7, p. 175).
**Correspondences.** Breast; pale, smoky, and silvery colours; aquatic creatures (Houlding).

### Leo

**Season.** Ptolemy: solid, the summer's heat felt most firmly (I.11).
Houlding: the Sun at the height of its ascent; the Nemean Lion as "the fierce and devastating summer heat that ravishes the land" (¶1–2).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: lion-wrestling heroes from Gilgamesh to Samson as stories of the solar year, and the Nemean Lion, whose hide no weapon could pierce (¶2).
**Temperament.** Houlding: every Leo needs a kingdom — a home, hobby, profession, or politics — to direct and be looked up to in; magnanimous when respected and fiercely defensive against rivals; extravagant; seeing the big picture and missing details (¶4–5, ¶8–9).
Valens: noble, steady, just, haters of flattery, and "inflated with their lofty thoughts" (p. 15).
Manilius: quick to anger and quick to relent, with a simple, sincere heart (IV.187–188).
**People.** Lilly: a great round head and large staring eyes; a full, large body with broad shoulders and narrow sides; curling yellow or dark flaxen hair; "a fierce countenance, but ruddy, high sanguine complexion, strong valiant and active" (pp. 95–96).
**Trades.** Manilius: hunters who hang pelts on their doorposts, and in towns, butchers (IV.178–186).
Houlding: leaders and rulers.
**Places.** Lilly: where wild beasts frequent; woods, forests, and deserts; steep, rocky, inaccessible places; kings' palaces, castles, forts, and parks; in houses, where fire is kept, near a chimney (p. 95).
Houlding adds theatres and grand public buildings, and rooms rich with velvet and gilt.
**Correspondences.** Heart and back; gold, orange, and royal colours; clawed animals (Houlding).

### Virgo

**Season.** Ptolemy: bicorporeal, carrying summer into autumn, where "the dry autumn is signified" (I.11; I.19).
Houlding: the herald of the harvest, a time for taking stock of resources and planning for scarcity; a sign of storage and cataloguing (¶3, ¶12–13).
**Emblem and myth.** The maiden, named Erigone by Manilius (IV.189) and "standing like the figure of Justice" in Valens (p. 16).
Ptolemy counts the sign winged (II.7), and Manilius makes its guardian Ceres, the wheat-ear bearer.
**Temperament.** Houlding: refined perception mistaken for fussiness; tidying and fixing small problems before they grow; a pragmatist preferring supporting roles in a worthwhile institution; impossible to buy (¶1, ¶6–9, ¶15).
Valens: modest, religious, and burdened with care (p. 16).
Manilius: a modesty that restrains great natural gifts (IV.200–201).
**People.** Lilly: slender and of middle height, with a ruddy brown complexion and black hair; "well-favoured or lovely, but no beautiful creature"; a small shrill voice; "a witty discreet soul, judicious and excellently well spoken, studious and given to History" (p. 96).
**Trades.** Valens: "administrators of others' goods, trusted, good stewards, secretaries, accountants, actors" (p. 16).
Manilius: the learned arts, eloquence, and shorthand writing (IV.190–199).
Houlding: housekeepers, analysts, herbalists, and makers of useful things (¶6, ¶11–12).
**Places.** Lilly: a study where books are; a closet; a dairy; cornfields, granaries, malt-houses, and hayricks; where cheese and butter are kept (p. 96).
Houlding adds pantries, cupboards, offices, sewing and repair spaces, and servants' quarters.
**Correspondences.** Belly; muted earthy colours; talkative birds (Houlding).

### Libra

**Season.** Ptolemy: equinoctial; autumn begins (I.11).
Houlding: anciently a time for taking stock, settling outstanding problems, and planning for the hard season; Michaelmas, a Quarter Day for paying dues and vacating properties (¶1–2, note 1).
Manilius: the scales balance night and day as the vintage ripens (IV.203–204).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: scales of judgement — the Egyptian heart weighed against the Feather of Truth, Astraea's balance, and the Archangel Michael weighing souls (¶2).
Manilius calls the sign *Chelae*, the Claws (IV.203), and poses it resting with its labours done (II.244–255); its guardian Vulcan forged the scales.
**Temperament.** Houlding: comparison and fair judgement; diplomacy and charm; indecision and avoidance of closure; getting what they want by negotiation; at best "the faithful equalisers" who take a stand (¶3–7, ¶10–13).
Valens: noble and just, but covetous of others' goods (p. 17).
Manilius: anything left in doubt, the Scales will settle (IV.215–216).
**People.** Lilly: straight, tall, and slender; a round, lovely face; a clear sanguine colour in youth and a high colour in age; yellowish, smooth, long hair (pp. 96–97).
**Trades.** Manilius: keepers of weights and measures and of law, each such person a magistrate at his own doorstep (IV.205–212).
Valens: the sign is "the supervisor of crops, vineyards, olive groves, aromatics, homesteads, measures, and artisans", and its natives are "in charge of measures, posts, and the grain supply" (p. 17).
Houlding: diplomats, negotiators, the welcoming face of a business, the arts, law, and government (¶15–16).
**Places.** Lilly: ground near windmills; a straggling barn or outhouse; saw-pits and places where coopers work or wood is cut; hillsides and mountaintops; hawking and hunting grounds; in houses, upper rooms, garrets, and "one Chamber within another" (p. 96).
Houlding adds places of worship and of civilised behaviour.
**Correspondences.** Kidneys and loins; greys and pastels; birds (Houlding).

### Scorpio

**Season.** Ptolemy: solid, the autumn felt most firmly (I.11).
Manilius: when the Sun is in Scorpio, men furrow the earth and sow (IV.218–219).
Houlding: an "autumnal sign", withdrawing from the surface to the root, where destruction and creativity merge (¶3, ¶10).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: scorpion gods as guardians, ferocious to anyone who breaks their watch; a scorpion that waits months to strike; the eagle as the sign's higher image; the Nile's black mud as the root of alchemy (¶5–6, ¶11, ¶15).
Manilius: pressed to the ground, its claws lent to Libra (II.244–264).
**Temperament.** Houlding: falsity against truth, since those able to deceive are best placed to spot deception; candour to the point of pain; rarely the attacker, defence being the strongest attack; secretive, loyal, and unforgetting; pulling strings behind the scenes (¶3–5, ¶17–19).
Valens gives a harsh list: tricky, thieving, treacherous, conniving, and hating their own families (p. 18).
Manilius: minds on fire for war (IV.220–222).
Lilly: "usually it doth represent subtill, deceitfull men" (p. 97).
**People.** Lilly: a corpulent, strong body; a broad, square face; a dusky complexion; dark, thick, crisp hair; a short neck; "a squat, wel-trussed Fellow" (p. 97).
**Trades.** Manilius: soldiers, hunters, and arena fighters (IV.222–229).
Houlding: military leadership, boardroom politics, and courtroom disputes (¶6).
**Places.** Lilly: where creeping things live; gardens, orchards, and vineyards; ruined houses near water; muddy ground, stinking lakes, quagmires, and sinks; the kitchen, larder, and wash-house (p. 97).
Houlding adds drains, cellars, houses awaiting renovation, compost heaps, and the sunless north wall, and reports Al-Biruni's prisons and places of mourning.
**Correspondences.** Groin; a dark blood-red brown; reptiles and poisonous insects (Houlding).

### Sagittarius

**Season.** Ptolemy: bicorporeal, carrying autumn into winter, with windy weather (I.11; II.11).
Manilius: the centaur's human front holds mild autumn while his beast's back meets winter (II.181–196).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: the centaur aiming upward at the distant horizon; the gentle healer Chiron set against the sign's fierce centaur; the Babylonian archer with a human face forward and an animal face behind; perhaps the Greeks' first sight of mounted archers (¶2, ¶4–8).
**Temperament.** Houlding: broad vision, love of freedom, and plenty over thrift; honesty that is "frank, blunt, and disquietingly valid"; quick to argue and quick to forgive; unable to keep a secret; restless with routine; "a belligerent foundation that is rarely acknowledged but ever-present" (¶1–4, ¶9–11).
Valens: noble, just, generous, loving brothers and friends, seeking a noble reputation, and acting mysteriously (p. 19).
Manilius: strong limbs, a sharp mind, swift movement, and a tireless heart (IV.240–242).
**People.** Lilly: well-favoured, with a long, full, ruddy or sunburnt face, light chestnut hair, and a strong, well-proportioned body somewhat above middle height (p. 98).
**Trades.** Manilius: chariot drivers, horse-breakers, herders, and trainers of beasts, even elephants (IV.231–237).
Houlding: teachers, counsellors, and entertainers; lovers of sport and travel (¶13–14).
**Places.** Lilly: stables of great horses or war-horses; hills and the highest ground; in houses, upper rooms near the fire (p. 98).
Houlding adds, for the sign's wild last half, uncultivated land where wild animals roam.
**Correspondences.** Thighs; vivid red, purple, and royal blue; horses, mules, and asses (Houlding).

### Capricorn

**Season.** Ptolemy: solstitial; the Sun turns and winter begins (I.11).
Manilius: it draws back the longest nights and starts the year with returning light (IV.253–255).
Houlding: mid-winter, the Sun's fire low and gestating; the Romans' Saturnalia welcomed the Sun back (¶3–4).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: a goat-fish spanning the ocean depths and the mountain heights; the goat Amalthea, who nursed Jupiter, and the horn of plenty; horns as crowns; Ea, the antelope of the ocean and the wise old sage (¶1, ¶4–5, ¶8–10, ¶13).
Manilius: the goat sits hunched against the frost (II.244–255), and Vesta, its guardian, gives the sign every craft that needs fire (IV.243–246).
**Temperament.** Houlding: "patient, prudent, determined, reliable and disciplined"; ambition held back by restraint; older than their years; wanting respect more than wealth and caring what the neighbours say; the preserver, not the innovator (¶1–3, ¶7, ¶16–17).
Valens gives a harsh list: warped, feigning sincerity, toilsome, sleepless, fond of jokes, and always criticising (pp. 20–21).
Manilius: changeable, with a restless mind (IV.256–258).
**People.** Lilly: a dry body, not tall; a long, lean face, thin beard, black hair, narrow chin, long thin neck, and narrow chest (p. 98).
Houlding: the parent who runs the household with structure and teaches delayed gratification; the unapproachable manager always in control (¶14).
**Trades.** Manilius: miners and smelters, gold- and silversmiths, smiths, bakers, and sellers of goods against the cold (IV.246–253).
Valens lists "a sculptor, a farmer" among the sign's epithets (p. 20).
**Places.** Lilly: ox-houses and cow-houses; stores of husbandry tools, old wood, or ships' sails; sheep-pens; fallow, barren, thorny fields; dunghills; in houses, low dark places near the ground or threshold (p. 98).
Houlding adds boundaries — walls, fences, gateposts — churchyards and ruins, docks, mines, and factories.
**Correspondences.** Knees; black, dark brown, and pale, lustreless shades; goats and herded animals (Houlding).

### Aquarius

**Season.** Ptolemy: solid, the winter's cold felt most firmly (I.11).
Houlding: floods at the sign's culmination were welcomed as cleansing, and "The month of February consequently takes its name from the ceremonies of purification" (¶3).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: the title of the royal officer who attended the king's bath; Babylonian Ea, the Great Man, with streams flowing from his fingers; Ganymede the cupbearer; the libation as purification and as passing on knowledge (¶1–5).
Manilius: youthful Aquarius pours a stream from a tilted urn (IV.259–260).
**Temperament.** Houlding: modest but fiercely independent of mind; speaking against the common drift; a detached reasoner who turns groups from personal quarrels to shared problems; preferring reasoned reform to disruption; "Aquarians tend to keep a cool head in all things" (¶6–7, ¶9–10, ¶12).
Valens gives a harsh list — self-willed, concealing everything, misanthropic — with natives "occasionally generous (because of <this sign's> flow of water)" (p. 21).
Manilius: gentle, not mean, and easily led into losses (IV.270–271).
**People.** Lilly: squat and thick, or strong and well composed, not tall, with a long face; with Saturn strong, black hair, otherwise a fair complexion and sandy or flaxen hair (p. 99).
**Trades.** Manilius: water engineers who find springs, raise fountains, build into the sea, and make artificial lakes, and makers of models of the heavens (IV.261–268).
Valens: "the cause of troubles arising from athletic training, carrying burdens, or work in hard materials, an artisan" (p. 21).
Houlding: teachers, engineers, and researchers; she reports Al-Biruni's makers of glass and jewellery (¶14–15).
**Places.** Lilly: hilly, uneven places; newly dug ground, quarries, and mines; in houses, roofs and eaves; vineyards; near a little spring or conduit head (p. 99).
Houlding adds attics, stairways, fountains, and water supplies.
**Correspondences.** Shins and ankles; sky blues and bluish greens (Houlding).

### Pisces

**Season.** Ptolemy: bicorporeal, carrying winter into spring, where "the beginning of the moist spring is indicated" (I.11; I.19).
Manilius: winter rains meet spring dews (II.181–196).
**Emblem and myth.** Houlding: two fish bound by a cord, swimming in different directions; fish as emblems of priests and of gentleness; the ocean from which everything comes and to which it returns (¶1, ¶3, ¶6, ¶14).
Valens: "in conflict with itself because one Fish is northern, the other southern" (p. 22).
**Temperament.** Houlding: "the sign of the poet and the mystic"; every situation a dilemma, with conscience and the fear of upsetting others outweighing self-interest; "If they see tears, they cry"; escaping rather than fighting; starting well but often failing to finish (¶2, ¶4, ¶7–8).
Valens: unsteady and popular (p. 22).
Manilius: friendly, quick in movement, and changing over a lifetime (IV.290–291).
Lilly calls it an idle sign, "representing a party of no action" (p. 99).
**People.** Lilly: short, "ill composed, not very decent", with a good large face, a pale complexion, and a fleshy body stooping at the head (p. 99).
**Trades.** Manilius: sailors, shipwrights, pilots who know coasts and winds, rowers, and fishers with nets and traps (IV.274–289).
Houlding: priests, artists, and the caring professions (¶3, ¶10).
**Places.** Lilly: ground full of water and springs, with much wildfowl; fishponds and rivers full of fish; where hermitages have been; moats; water-mills; in houses, near a well or pump, or where water stands (p. 99).
Houlding adds monasteries, sanctuaries, and retreats.
**Correspondences.** Feet; sea-green and pale glittering colours; aquatic creatures (Houlding).

## I — Self

**Source notes:** Heese associates personal appeal with approval-seeking and resentment.
AstroLibrary adds self-presentation that tells others what they want to hear.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can enjoy being encountered through something he has deliberately chosen about his own presence.
One expression is uncomplicated pleasure in being attractive, without needing either reward or apology.
Another is irritation at becoming so agreeable that nobody expects him to have a preference.
Both concern how he arrives bodily and socially; public credit belongs elsewhere.

**Scene A:** A tailor lends him a startling red lining, visible only when he turns; he can try the unfamiliar flourish or choose a quieter pleasure in the fit.
**Scene B:** A host keeps introducing him as someone who never objects; he can savor the welcome, correct the description, or introduce himself afresh to the next guest.

## II — Livelihood

**Source notes:** Heese connects tangible affection, taste, and gifts anticipating returns.
AstroLibrary emphasizes spending on pleasurable possessions.

**Original adaptation:** Resources become worth having through the life they let the Prince actually enjoy.
One expression is spending on a small delight because tomorrow's provisions already suffice.
Another is wanting visible acknowledgement that he supplied something valuable.
Possession can be satisfying; generosity can contain an expectation without becoming a moral verdict.
Make the resource his to use or transfer, rather than another person's estate.

**Scene A:** His travel allowance covers either ordinary soap and a replacement strap or one fragrant bar he has wanted since the last town; both purchases are intelligible.
**Scene B:** A fruit seller accepts his overpayment as a gift and immediately serves the best peach to someone else; he can enjoy having provided it or ask for the peach himself.

## III — Communication

**Source notes:** Heese combines verbal attraction, flattery, and playful misdirection.
AstroLibrary locates affectionate expression among siblings and neighbors.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can want an everyday exchange to carry a particular person's attention.
One expression is the pleasure of making a familiar listener laugh.
Another is choosing a flattering version of events because he wants to remain the favored correspondent.
Language here makes local relations pleasurable or charged; it need not settle an argument or demonstrate cleverness.

**Scene A:** His sister asks him to finish a teasing market rhyme about her new admirer; he can make it affectionate, sharpen it, or confess he envies the attention.
**Scene B:** A neighbor repeats his compliment every time they meet, using precisely the same words; he can repeat the ritual gladly or risk offering something more particular today.

## IV — Home

**Source notes:** Heese emphasizes domestic affection and attachment.
AstroLibrary adds enjoyment in hosting people at home.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can want a household to feel like somewhere his particular affections have room.
One expression is delight in preparing a welcome suited to someone he knows.
Another is possessiveness about who belongs inside a familiar room or ritual.
The emphasis is on cherished domestic relations, while Moon IV below explores the felt recognition of belonging.
Neither requires a happy childhood or an assigned maternal figure.

**Scene A:** Returning home, he finds his favorite courtyard prepared for a relative's new companion; he can join their supper or ask for the old place beside the tree.
**Scene B:** The household is choosing which dish to cook on its last evening together; he can ask for the one everyone praises or the unfashionable one someone used to make for him.

## V — Creativity

**Source notes:** Heese describes playful sensuality and romantic attention.
AstroLibrary adds performance, hobbies, and enjoyment with children.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can want to follow what delights him long enough for it to become a shared occasion.
One expression is absorbed enjoyment without any claim that it improves him.
Another is the particular thrill of being chosen by the person whose attention he wants.
Artistic accomplishment is optional; play, desire, and conspicuous frivolity also belong.
Venus's joy supplies no requirement that he be cheerful or naturally gifted.

**Scene A:** Two festival guests invite him into an intentionally ridiculous dance; he can enjoy the absurdity, draw one guest closer, or watch with unabashed pleasure.
**Scene B:** Children let him design the villain's costume for their puppet play; he can make it extravagantly beautiful or delight them with a hideous, rattling hat.

## VI — Labor

**Source notes:** Heese presents affection through practical availability.
AstroLibrary explicitly includes improving coworkers' working conditions.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince notices what would make necessary work bearable for a particular person, including himself.
One expression is satisfaction in a considerate arrangement that works.
Another is resentment when affection is measured by how readily he accepts another task.
The scene should name the task, its physical burden, and who can change its conditions.
Care need not become institutional reform or compulsory self-sacrifice.

**Scene A:** A laundry worker has warmed water for his cracked hands before their next load; he can accept the care, share it, or insist she take the first turn.
**Scene B:** Someone he likes asks him to finish their cleaning again while they rest; he can gladly take this turn or say he wants their company after a shared finish.

## VII — Relationships

**Source notes:** Heese emphasizes partnership and costly accommodation.
AstroLibrary includes demanding an ideal relationship.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can want the terms of being together to express what he actually enjoys about this person.
One expression is delight in inventing an arrangement both can inhabit.
Another is disappointment that a desired partner declines an apparently perfect offer.
Affection gives negotiation its stakes; it does not guarantee agreement or oblige anyone to accept.
The other's stated wants should remain specific and credible.

**Scene A:** A companion wants separate rooms but supper together; he can accept that shape of closeness or explain why sharing the evening alone feels insufficient.
**Scene B:** A rival offers a joint performance, provided neither takes the bow alone; he can enjoy their mutual admiration, bargain for alternating introductions, or decline the arrangement.

## VIII — Transformation

**Source notes:** Heese stresses vulnerability and consuming attachment.
AstroLibrary includes comfort through a partner's resources or inheritance.
Reed explicitly adds alimony and gain through partnership.
Houlding's house discussion centers loss and financial obligations, separating these from subsequent growth.

**Original adaptation:** What the Prince cherishes can become inseparable from what must be divided, relinquished, or settled after a loss.
One expression is tenderness toward a remnant without demanding that it heal him.
Another is wanting the most intimate claim recognized during a material settlement.
This joins distinct source emphases; no source inspected establishes the specific behavior below.

**Scene A:** An executor offers his late companion's cloak as part of his share; he can take it, exchange that share for money, or let another mourner keep it.
**Scene B:** A former partner offers to settle their remaining joint debt by selling the silver they chose together; he can agree or surrender his own purse to keep one cup.

## IX — Pilgrimage

**Source notes:** Heese emphasizes attraction across cultures and relational freedom.
AstroLibrary adds foreign arts, ceremony, and learning.

**Original adaptation:** Attraction can make the Prince willing to approach a distant practice before he possesses an explanation for it.
One expression is curiosity drawn by an unfamiliar sound or form.
Another is longing to travel with someone whose life cannot be brought neatly into his own.
Pleasure can initiate participation without converting him, authorizing exotic stereotypes, or proving a creed true.

**Scene A:** At a distant shrine, he loves the evening chant but cannot endorse its words; he can hum its melody, listen, or ask what joining would mean to the singers.
**Scene B:** A traveling musician offers him one lesson before her boat leaves; he can learn enough to remember the tune or spend their remaining time talking about where she is going.

## XI — Friendship

**Source notes:** Heese links attraction with friendship and group affiliation.
AstroLibrary emphasizes congenial company and friends' support.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can want companionship that makes a shared undertaking enjoyable in itself.
One expression is easy delight in a circle where he is already welcome.
Another is jealousy when a favored friend gives someone else the attention he expected.
The group has several genuine relationships, rather than behaving as one approving audience.
A gift from friends can remain a gift, without invented future debt.

**Scene A:** Friends divide preparations for an evening meal and leave him the job they know he enjoys; he can accept with pleasure or join another task for someone's company.
**Scene B:** His usual partner at a communal game has chosen a newcomer; he can make a new pairing, watch them play, or admit he had been looking forward to their familiar rhythm.

## XII — The Hidden

**Source notes:** Heese emphasizes concealed affection and sacrifice.
Reed foregrounds desired solitude; AstroLibrary likewise allows private enjoyment.
Cunningham's reply to Carol proposes befriending isolated people and making art.
Houlding supplies concrete confinement and denied public access.

**Original adaptation:** Affection or pleasure can matter where contact is restricted, or where the Prince chooses privacy on intelligible terms.
One expression is preserving a private delight without wanting an audience.
Another is wanting contact with someone an institution keeps apart, while resenting the limits imposed on their time.
These settings are our construction; neither hidden emotion alone nor Pisces symbolism establishes the house.

**Scene A:** A secluded retreat has one room where guests may sing alone; he can take it gladly or invite a permitted visitor to listen from the doorway.
**Scene B:** A detained friend requests a familiar scent on the cloth allowed through the visiting grille; he can prepare it, use their short visit for conversation, or admit he wants to be remembered too.

## Independent overlap — Moon IV

**Source notes:** Annie allows restlessness within the search for belonging and attachment to family history.
AstroLibrary similarly connects remembered objects and changing residences with security.
Their agreement is not evidence that a natal placement causes either behavior.

**Original adaptation:** The Prince can recognize a home through what his body expects there, even when that expectation no longer matches the household.
One expression is relief at an ordinary routine that requires no explanation.
Another is an urge to move things or leave because a supposedly familiar place feels wrong.
He need not discover that the problem was inside him, as the source's corrective framing suggests.
The room or relationship may actually have changed.

**Scene A:** A cousin has replaced the old breakfast bell; he can ask to hear the cracked one again or discover whether the new sound can belong to this morning.
**Scene B:** His hosts offer the room where he slept as a child, but he wants the unfamiliar attic; he can take it without explaining himself or tell them what the old room recalls.

## Remaining research gaps

Most direct coverage remains modern psychological interpretation; this is not a survey of traditional natal delineation.
Reed broadens VIII and XII; AstroLibrary's imported-text provenance limits claims of independent agreement.
Cunningham's nonromantic XII examples are useful alternatives, while her Pisces rationale is excluded rather than silently adopted.
The overlap's useful uncertainty is whether staying, rearranging, or leaving answers a real household change; no automatic moral resolution is warranted.
Pleasure, hospitality, envy, grief, and refusal all remain available writing directions, pending scene testing.

# Prose and voice

In authored encounters the narrator is plain, and the people in a scene hold the convictions.
Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's [*The Little Prince*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Little_Prince) is the reference for the shape of the prose: short episodes, a narrator who reports, and speakers who say what they believe.
Kahlil Gibran's *The Prophet* (1923) is the reference for what a speaker may believe: a precise claim about an ordinary thing, which the listener is free to dispute.

## Keep the narrator plain

The narrator reports who is here and what they do and say.
In *The Little Prince* the lamplighter lights and puts out his lamp once a minute; the narrator describes the work, and the judgment of that life is left to the visitor.

Use familiar words and modern English.
Use second person and present tense for narrative openings.
Leave reflection to the speakers and the player: the narrator does not say what a scene means.
An unanswered request or an action still possible can end the scene.
Read aloud for ease and continuity, and vary sentence shapes across a batch.

## Give speakers their convictions

> It is the time you have wasted for your rose that makes your rose so important.

The fox says this to the little prince.
The claim is the fox's, and the reader may weigh it.

Give dialogue its own manners and convictions.
A conviction can be large or small, generous or self-serving, right or wrong; an uncle who says the wood is dry holds one too.
Formal turns of phrase belong to a speaker or moment that suits them.
Leave room for tenderness, impatience, embarrassment, and uncomplicated rest.
A line of Gibran's weight suits some scenes, not most, and speakers should not be uniformly wise.

The passages below show three ways a speaker's claim earns its place.

### Make a precise observation

> You may give them your love but not your thoughts,
> For they have their own thoughts.

Gibran distinguishes parental affection from possession of a child's thinking, then gives a plain reason for the distinction. [“On Children”](https://poets.org/poem/children-1)

Let a speaker say what a desire, act, or relationship means in this particular situation, with their reason.
The listener can understand the claim and question it.

### Make the paradox intelligible

> And what is fear of need but need itself?
> Is not dread of thirst when your well is full, the thirst that is unquenchable?

The full well distinguishes material provision from the fear of lacking it.
The question gives an apparently contradictory claim a concrete meaning. [“On Giving”](https://poets.org/poem/giving)

Let a surprising claim expose a tension the reader can follow, and give the tension evidence in the scene.
A rhetorical question should express something this speaker wants to ask, challenge, or recognize.

### Give ordinary work personal meaning

> It is to build a house with affection, even as if your beloved were to dwell in that house.

The imagined inhabitant makes care in the work personal and specific. [“On Work”](https://poets.org/poem/work-4)

Give people particular uses for what they want and particular reasons to care.
Write with affection, candor, and room for humor.
A scene of pleasure can be complete in what the characters enjoy.

## Keep cadence in dialogue

Balanced clauses, purposeful repetition, and an image followed through belong to a speaker who would talk that way.
In narration they supply rhythm without a claim.

## Leave the Prince to the player

Keep the Prince's feelings and interpretation available to the player.
Characters can express beliefs the player questions; their conviction does not make those beliefs the game's verdict.
Describe the Prince's actions without assigning a motive to the choice.

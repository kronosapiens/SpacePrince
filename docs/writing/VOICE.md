# Prose and voice

Use a Gibran-inspired tone for authored encounter narration and dialogue: intimate, musical, and clear about what matters to the people involved.
Kahlil Gibran's *The Prophet* (1923) is the main prose reference; [*Siddhartha*](../intent.md#siddhartha-hermann-hesse) guides learning through experience.
The passages below demonstrate the techniques to adapt.
The [research record](../research/NARRATIVE_VOICE.md#gibran-and-encounter-prose) retains scenario comparisons and reader feedback.

## Make a precise observation

> You may give them your love but not your thoughts,
> For they have their own thoughts.

Gibran distinguishes parental affection from possession of a child's thinking, then gives a plain reason for the distinction. [“On Children”](https://poets.org/poem/children-1)

Give a passage something the reader can understand and question.
Identify what a desire, act, or relationship means in this particular situation.
The thought need not become an explicit lesson.

## Give repetition a purpose

> Fill each other’s cup but drink not from one cup.
> Give one another of your bread but eat not from the same loaf.

The repeated sentence shape joins generosity with separateness; each familiar action gives the same relationship another expression. [“On Marriage”](https://poets.org/poem/marriage-3)

Use parallel clauses to bring desires, costs, or perspectives into relation.
Repeat a word or clause opening when each return gives the reader another concrete way to understand the thought.
Let longer sentences carry qualifications and shorter ones give an action or admission its weight.
Read aloud for ease and continuity, and vary sentence shapes across a batch.

## Develop the image

> Your house is your larger body.
> It grows in the sun and sleeps in the stillness of the night; and it is not dreamless.

The second sentence follows the comparison into growth and sleep, giving the house a life continuous with its inhabitant's. [“On Houses”](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58585/58585-h/58585-h.htm)

Choose an image whose workings help explain the situation.
Follow what it can do, need, or undergo before introducing another image.
Personification should make a relationship or experience recognizable.
Keep the practical request clear enough that interpreting the imagery is optional.

## Make the paradox intelligible

> And what is fear of need but need itself?
> Is not dread of thirst when your well is full, the thirst that is unquenchable?

The full well distinguishes material provision from the fear of lacking it.
The question gives an apparently contradictory claim a concrete meaning. [“On Giving”](https://poets.org/poem/giving)

Let a surprising observation expose a tension the reader can follow.
Give the tension evidence in the scene.
A rhetorical question should express something this speaker wants to ask, challenge, or recognize; it need not supply the ending of every encounter.

## Give ordinary work personal meaning

> It is to build a house with affection, even as if your beloved were to dwell in that house.

The imagined inhabitant makes care in the work personal and specific. [“On Work”](https://poets.org/poem/work-4)

Give people particular uses for what they want and particular reasons to care.
Details earn attention through a relationship, practical condition, or pleasure.
Write with affection, candor, and room for humor.
A scene of pleasure can be complete in what the characters enjoy.

## Adapt the voice to an encounter

Use familiar words and modern English.
Formal turns of phrase belong to a speaker or moment that suits them.
Use second person and present tense for narrative openings, and give dialogue its own manners and convictions.
Let the shared cadence leave room for tenderness, impatience, embarrassment, and uncomplicated rest.

Where the scene supports an observation, the narrator may state it directly.
Reflection can reveal why someone cares or connect details that might pass unnoticed.
When the action already supplies the thought, leave it room.
An unanswered request or an action still possible can end the scene.

Keep the Prince's feelings and interpretation available to the player.
Characters can express beliefs the player questions; their conviction does not make those beliefs the game's verdict.
Describe the Prince's actions without assigning a motive to the choice.

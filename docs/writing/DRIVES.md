# Drives and emotional range

Working authoring guidance for giving the Prince personal wants and the [house–planet mappings](HOUSE_PLANET_MATRIX.md) greater emotional range.
The vocabulary and examples are proposals to develop through research and scene writing.

## The Prince and the player

The Prince's motives belong to the fiction; the player's motivations and emotional experience belong to playing the game.
They can influence one another without matching.
A Prince seeking recognition might act from envy while the player feels curiosity, sympathy, discomfort, or satisfaction at finding an effective choice.
Research about player experience can help us evaluate encounters, but it does not directly supply a taxonomy of the Prince's drives.

## Working vocabulary

For authoring, distinguish:

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drive | A broad concern that can recur across situations | Recognition |
| Want | Something the Prince seeks in this encounter | Public acknowledgement of his contribution |
| Emotion | A feeling arising around the situation and what matters to him | Pride, envy, embarrassment |
| Response | What he does about it | Claim credit, share it, conceal his concern, withdraw |

These distinctions help turn an abstract concern into a playable situation.
They leave room for conflicting wants and for the same action to have different motives.

The following drives are a starting vocabulary chosen for this project.
They overlap and can be expanded or revised; their number has no connection to the eight categories in either research framework below.

| Drive | What the Prince might seek |
| --- | --- |
| Recognition | To be seen, respected, admired, or taken seriously |
| Security | Safety, continuity, protection from loss, or dependable support |
| Belonging | Affection, intimacy, acceptance, or a place among others |
| Autonomy | Freedom to choose, refuse, leave, or act without permission |
| Pleasure | Enjoyment, beauty, play, comfort, or sensory delight |
| Possession | To acquire, keep, or exercise control over something valued |
| Understanding | Knowledge, explanation, discovery, or relief from uncertainty |
| Purpose | Meaning, commitment, or something worth giving oneself to |

Giving a gift might express affection, seek gratitude, or establish a claim on its recipient.
A desire for security might prompt preparation, dependence, concealment, or refusal.
The scene makes the motive specific.

## Emotional range

The Prince can be generous, vain, frightened, resentful, curious, possessive, tender, or simply enjoying himself.
Motives can coexist: generosity can include wanting gratitude; duty can include fear of becoming unnecessary.
Self-deception can create a gap between the reason he gives and what his response suggests.
The writing can make that gap legible while leaving the player room to interpret it.

Every planet should admit difficult feelings as well as welcome ones.
Each planet can express several drives, and no placement fixes a moral personality.
A placement makes a concern available; choices let the player decide how the Prince responds.
Pleasure can remain pleasurable, resentment unresolved, and refusal understandable.
An encounter need not correct the Prince or reward the most virtuous interpretation.
Likewise, emotional breadth does not require a hidden downside in every gift or a good and bad version of every entry.

Hermann Hesse's *Siddhartha* offers a literary reference for changing attachments: ways of living can become attractive, consuming, and eventually insufficient.
For Space Prince, this suggests allowing different wants to matter at different moments, including wants that contradict earlier choices.
It does not prescribe a biography or a sequence of moral advancement.

## Applying this to houses and planets

Use the [house reference](HOUSE_MATRIX.md) for situations and stakes, the [planet reference](PLANET_MATRIX.md#planet-frameworks) for concerns and forms of agency, and signs for their expression.
Research the pairing before assigning it a drive; the vocabulary should help articulate what is found.
Several planets can seek recognition, but the reason it matters and the means of seeking it should give each pairing character.
Changing the house should change what is at stake, beyond exchanging the scene's objects.

When developing an entry, ask:

- What might the Prince want here, and what makes that want specific to both the house and the planet?
- What different motives or responses could the same dynamic support?
- Can it generate substantially different scenes, including pleasure, difficulty, or unresolved feeling where those fit?
- What might the player find engaging, and how could their experience differ from the Prince's?

These are drafting and review questions, not required fields for every entry.
Keep source fidelity, usefulness for writing scenes, and eventual player experience as separate assessments.
Fresh scene writing can test whether an interpretation is productive; playtesting is needed to assess the experience it actually produces.
A literary burden does not establish an economic cost: mechanics and prices remain a separate design task.

## Research basis

The following passages were consulted on 2026-10-04.
Their contributions are distinct; the vocabulary and authoring guidance above are our synthesis.

- **Hermann Hesse, [*Siddhartha*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2500/pg2500-images.html) (1922), passages in “The Son of the Brahman,” “Sansara,” and “The Son.”**
  Spiritual dissatisfaction, absorption in wealth and pleasure, and attachment to his son provide literary examples of changing and conflicting wants.
  Use this range as inspiration for characterization.
- **Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, and Robert Zubek, [*MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research*](https://cdn.aaai.org/Workshops/2004/WS-04-04/WS04-04-001.pdf) (2004), p. 2, “Aesthetics.”**
  Its non-exhaustive vocabulary of player experience includes sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, and submission.
  Use it to ask what an encounter offers the player; these are not eight character drives or a list attributable to Koster.
- **Raph Koster, [“Sorts of fun, sorts of kfun?”](https://www.raphkoster.com/2005/11/01/sorts-of-fun-sorts-of-kfun/) (2005), discussion following his definition of fun; [“Influences”](https://www.raphkoster.com/games/presentations/influences/) (2006), the Sims example through the discussion of good-versus-evil stories.**
  The first distinguishes learning from aesthetic, bodily, and social responses; the second challenges simplified representations of human experience and encourages broader artistic influences.
  Use these to ask what the player can discover through choices and whether our representations are too narrow.
  Learning through play does not require the Prince to learn a moral lesson.
- **Jukka Vahlo and Kai Tuuri, [“Eight types of video game experience”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.entcom.2024.100882) (2025), §§2.1, 5.1, and 6.1; [consulted full text](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/187384/1-s2.0-S1875952124002507-main.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1).**
  A UK/US survey identifies eight patterns of valued game experience, including experiences involving melancholy, fear, and anxiety.
  This supports considering a wider emotional range when evaluating play, without establishing that adding negative feelings improves an encounter.
  The categories concern experiences, not universal drives or fixed player types; structured self-report and geographic coverage limit generalization.
